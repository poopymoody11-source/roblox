# 1. Rip Van Winkle (Washington Irving, 1819)

## Main characters
**Rip Van Winkle (protagonist)**
- **Who:** a good-natured villager from a once-brave Dutch family, living at the foot of the Catskills under British rule.
- **Traits:** kind, generous, loved by kids and neighbors, but has "an insuperable aversion to all kinds of profitable labor" (35). He'll help anyone else with hard work but neglects his own farm and children.
- **Conflict:** person vs. person (his wife) and person vs. society (the new, changed America).
- **Change:** **static**. He's just as idle after 20 years, but now he's old enough that idleness is accepted.
- **Big moment:** "I'm not myself—I'm somebody else" (47). He loses his identity when his world disappears.
- **Represents:** the old, easygoing colonial life; possibly Irving himself, who lived off his brothers and failed in business.

**Dame Van Winkle (Rip's wife)**
- **Who:** a "termagant" (scolding wife) whose "tongue was incessantly going."
- **Traits:** hardworking, angry, controlling. She's right that Rip neglects the family, but she's shown only through Rip's side.
- **Type:** **flat**, stereotyped, a **foil** to Rip (duty vs. laziness).
- **Fate:** dies after bursting a blood vessel in a rage at a peddler.
- **Represents:** strict rule, like King George. Rip calls her rule "petticoat government" and "despotism" (50).

## Side characters
- **Wolf:** Rip's dog, "as much henpecked as his master." Doubles our pity for Rip. When Rip returns, a dog like Wolf snarls at him, showing everything is lost.
- **Nicholas Vedder:** innkeeper and village "patriarch" who judged the news by how he puffed his pipe. Dead when Rip returns: the old leadership is gone.
- **Derrick Van Bummel:** schoolmaster who read old newspapers to the club. Becomes a general and congressman: the new America rewards ambition.
- **Brom Dutcher:** Rip's friend, killed in the war. The human cost of the Revolution.
- **Hendrick Hudson and his crew (ghosts):** silent Dutchmen in old-fashioned clothes playing ninepins; their game is the "thunder." The supernatural, Romantic element.
- **The stranger with the keg:** a short, old Dutchman who silently leads Rip up to the crew.
- **The man in the cocked hat:** a self-important new politician who demands "Federal or Democrat?" and calls Rip "A tory!" Satire of new politics.
- **Judith Gardenier:** Rip's grown daughter; recognizes him and takes him in. The happy ending.
- **Rip Jr.:** "a precise counterpart of himself," just as lazy.
- **Peter Vanderdonk:** the oldest villager and local historian; confirms the legend of Hudson's crew.
- **Diedrich Knickerbocker:** the fake narrator in the preface, Irving's hoax persona.

## Plot (detailed)
1. **Frame:** the tale was supposedly "found among the papers of the late Diedrich Knickerbocker."
2. **Exposition:** the magical Kaatskill mountains; the old Dutch village; Rip is lovable but lazy, and his farm and kids are a mess.
3. Dame nags constantly. Rip escapes to the inn club (Vedder, Van Bummel). Dame even breaks up the club.
4. **Rising action:** Rip flees to the mountains with his gun and Wolf. At sunset he hears "Rip Van Winkle!" called.
5. A strange old Dutchman asks for help carrying a keg up into a hollow.
6. There, silent men in old clothes play ninepins; the rolling balls sound like thunder.
7. Rip secretly drinks from the flagon again and again.
8. **Climax:** he falls into a deep sleep.
9. **Falling action:** he wakes: his gun is rusty, Wolf is gone, his beard is a foot long. He thinks it's the next morning.
10. The village is bigger and noisier; nobody knows him; his house is in ruins.
11. The inn is now the "Union Hotel." King George's face on the sign has been repainted as "GENERAL WASHINGTON."
12. A crowd argues politics. Asked "Federal or Democrat?", Rip says he's "a loyal subject of the King" and is almost mobbed as a Tory.
13. He asks for his friends: Vedder is dead, Dutcher was killed in the war, Van Bummel is in Congress.
14. He sees Rip Jr. and panics about who he is.
15. **Resolution:** Judith recognizes him. Dame is dead. Peter Vanderdonk explains Hudson's crew returns every 20 years.
16. Rip lives with Judith, sits at the inn, and tells his story to every newcomer; henpecked husbands wish for a drink from his flagon.

# 2. The Eve of St. Agnes (John Keats, 1819)

## Main characters
**Madeline**
- **Who:** the Baron's young daughter.
- **Traits:** pious, dreamy, innocent, obsessed with the St. Agnes legend. She ignores everyone at the party: "her heart was otherwhere" (71).
- **Arc:** she wants a dream lover; when she wakes to the real Porphyro she's disappointed: "How chang'd thou art! how pallid, chill, and drear!" (346). She still runs off with him.
- **Represents:** innocence, the dream world, religious devotion.

**Porphyro**
- **Who:** a young man from the family that hates Madeline's family.
- **Traits:** passionate ("with heart on fire / For Madeline," 85–86), brave enough to enter an enemy castle, but also sneaky: he uses a "stratagem," hides in her closet, and watches her undress.
- **Arc:** sneaks in, wins over Angela by guilt and pressure, wakes Madeline, and escapes with her.
- **Debate:** romantic hero or manipulative intruder? Angela calls him "A cruel man and impious" (156). Keats leaves it **ambiguous**.

## Side characters
- **Angela:** the old nurse ("beldame"), "weak in body and in soul." The only one kind to Porphyro. Warns him he'll be killed, refuses his plan, then gives in and hides him. Dies "palsy-twitch'd" at the end (418).
- **The Beadsman:** an old man paid to pray, counting his rosary in the freezing chapel: "Numb were the Beadsman's fingers" (7). Opens and closes the poem; dies "among his ashes cold" (420). The **frame**: age and death around young love.
- **The Baron:** Madeline's father; dangerous host of the party; has nightmares at the end ("dreamt of many a woe," 414).
- **Hildebrand and Lord Maurice:** Porphyro's enemies at the party, part of "the whole blood-thirsty race" (110).
- **The guests / "wassailers":** drunk partiers asleep by the end, letting the lovers escape.
- **The porter and the bloodhound:** guards the lovers sneak past at the door.

## Plot (detailed)
1. **Stanzas 1–3:** a bitter-cold St. Agnes' Eve; the Beadsman prays among stone statues and hears party music.
2. **4–5:** the castle fills with a loud, rich party.
3. **6–8:** the legend (girls who skip supper, lie on their backs, and don't look behind them will dream of their future husband). Madeline ignores the dance.
4. **9–11:** Porphyro sneaks in; finds Angela, who is terrified for him.
5. **12–14:** Angela warns him and reveals Madeline's plan; he nearly cries thinking of her.
6. **15–17:** he proposes a "stratagem" to hide in her room; Angela is horrified; he swears no harm and threatens to wake his enemies.
7. **18–21:** Angela agrees, leads him to Madeline's room, and hides him in the closet.
8. **22–27:** Madeline enters, prays in the moonlight through stained glass, undresses, and falls asleep.
9. **28–32:** Porphyro creeps out, sets out a feast of exotic foods, and plays "La belle dame sans mercy" on her lute.
10. **33–36 (climax):** she wakes, confused between dream and reality; he melts into her dream: "This is no dream, my bride, my Madeline!" (363).
11. **37–40:** a storm rises; he promises "a home for thee" (390); they sneak past the drunk guests, the porter, and the bloodhound.
12. **41–42 (resolution):** "they are gone: ay, ages long ago" (412). The Baron has nightmares; Angela dies; the Beadsman dies cold.

# 3. The Fall of the House of Usher (Edgar Allan Poe, 1839)

## Main characters
**The narrator**
- **Who:** an unnamed man, Roderick's "boon companion" from boyhood, who hasn't seen him in years.
- **Traits:** logical; tries to explain things scientifically ("merely electrical phenomena"), but slowly catches Roderick's fear.
- **Arc:** **dynamic**: from calm visitor to terrified witness who flees.
- **Note:** possibly **unreliable**; he admits his "superstition" is growing.

**Roderick Usher**
- **Who:** the last male Usher, owner of the house.
- **Traits:** "cadaverousness of complexion," "ghastly pallor"; senses painfully sharp (only bland food, no bright light, no flower smells); artist, musician, poet; terrified of fear itself.
- **Arc:** declines into madness; buries Madeline alive, hears her for days, confesses, and dies of terror when she appears.
- **Represents:** the dying family line and a mind collapsing, mirrored by the house and "The Haunted Palace."

**Madeline Usher**
- **Who:** Roderick's twin sister, his "last and only relative on earth."
- **Traits:** silent, ghostly, wasting away from a mysterious cataleptic disease (deathlike trances).
- **Arc:** appears once, seems to die, is entombed, breaks out with "blood upon her white robes," and falls dead on Roderick.
- **Represents:** the buried family secret returning; the other half of Roderick.

## Side characters
- **The house:** basically a character: "vacant eye-like windows," fungus, a zigzag crack. It "dies" with the family.
- **The valet:** the servant who leads the narrator through the dark halls.
- **The family physician:** has "an expression of low cunning and perplexity"; one reason Roderick keeps Madeline's body in the house.
- **Ethelred:** hero of "The Mad Trist," whose battle sounds echo Madeline's escape.
- **King Porphyrogene ("Thought"):** the ruler of "The Haunted Palace," who stands for Roderick's reason.

## Plot (detailed)
1. **Exposition:** on a dull autumn day the narrator arrives at the gloomy mansion beside a black tarn; he feels "insufferable gloom."
2. Roderick's desperate letter brought him; the Ushers have only one direct line of descent, so "House of Usher" means both family and building.
3. He notices a barely visible crack from the roof to the tarn.
4. **Rising action:** Roderick is shockingly changed; he describes his illness and fear. Madeline drifts past and vanishes; that night she takes to her bed.
5. Days pass with painting, reading, and music. Roderick sings "The Haunted Palace" and believes the house's stones are alive.
6. Roderick says Madeline is "no more" and wants to keep her body two weeks in a vault under the house.
7. They place her in the copper-lined vault. The narrator learns they were **twins** and notices a faint blush and lingering smile.
8. Over the next week, Roderick wanders in terror; the narrator feels it too.
9. **Climax:** on a stormy night, the narrator reads "The Mad Trist" aloud. Each sound (wood cracking, a dragon's shriek, a clanging shield) is echoed in the house.
10. Roderick confesses: "We have put her living in the tomb!"
11. The doors open; Madeline stands there, bloody; she falls on Roderick, and both die.
12. **Resolution:** the narrator flees; the crack widens under a blood-red moon; the house splits and sinks into the tarn.

# 4. Baker's Bluejay Yarn (Mark Twain, 1880)

## Main characters
**Jim Baker**
- **Who:** "a middle-aged, simple-hearted miner who had lived in a lonely corner of California, among the woods and mountains, a good many years" (137).
- **Traits:** lonely, folksy, a storyteller; says he understands animal talk, especially bluejays.
- **Represents:** the leftover frontier miner after the Gold Rush; a possibly unreliable tall-tale teller.

**The first bluejay**
- **Traits:** proud, stubborn, hardworking, hot-tempered; swears a lot.
- **Arc:** finds a knothole, drops acorns for two and a half hours, gets furious, and becomes the joke of all jays.
- **Represents:** human stubbornness and pointless effort (your note: like the Gold Rush).

## Side characters
- **The narrator:** the frame voice who introduces Baker.
- **The other jays (about 5,000):** argue with "leather-headed opinions" like "an average crowd of humans," then laugh for an hour. Satire of crowds.
- **The old jay:** the one who finally looks through the half-open door and discovers the acorns on the floor.
- **The owl from Nova Scotia:** the only one who didn't find it funny; he "was a good deal disappointed about Yo Semite, too." The final punchline.
- **The cabin's former owner:** "the last man in this region," who left seven years earlier.

## Plot (detailed)
1. **Frame:** the narrator says animals talk and introduces Baker.
2. Baker's speech: jays have moods, great vocabulary, swearing, gossip, humor, and "no more principle than a Congressman."
3. **Setting:** seven years ago the last neighbor left; his log cabin has one room and "no ceiling."
4. **Rising action:** one Sunday a jay with an acorn finds a knothole in the roof and drops it in.
5. He can't see where it went, so he keeps dropping acorns, getting angrier.
6. After two and a half hours he's exhausted: "acorns enough... to keep the family thirty years."
7. Another jay comes, then more, until about 5,000 argue about the hole.
8. **Turning point:** an old jay looks through the half-open door and sees the acorns "scattered all over the floor."
9. **Resolution:** all the jays laugh for an hour; for three years jays come to see it, except the owl, who doesn't get it.

# 5. Young Goodman Brown (Nathaniel Hawthorne, 1835)

## Main characters
**Goodman Brown**
- **Who:** a young Puritan in Salem village, married only three months. "Goodman" was a polite title, like "Mr.," but it also hints at "a good man," or **everyman**.
- **Traits:** sure of his own goodness at first, curious about evil, proud of his "honest" Puritan family.
- **Arc:** **dynamic**: from hopeful and trusting to "a stern, a sad, a darkly meditative, a distrustful, if not a desperate man" (68). He dies in gloom.
- **Represents:** every person tempted by evil who then can't trust anyone.

**Faith Brown**
- **Who:** his new wife, with pink ribbons in her cap.
- **Traits:** sweet, worried; begs him to stay home ("Pray tarry with me this night").
- **Double entendre:** Faith = his wife **and** his religious faith. "Faith kept me back a while" (55); "My Faith is gone!" (62).
- **At the meeting:** she appears as a convert. Whether she resisted is never answered.

**The traveler (the devil)**
- **Who:** a man of about fifty who looks like Brown's grandfather (or father).
- **Traits:** calm, well-spoken, at ease anywhere; his staff looks like "a great black snake."
- **Actions:** claims he helped Brown's grandfather whip a Quaker woman and his father burn an Indian village; knows the deacons, selectmen, and governor; gives Goody Cloyse his staff; leads the witch meeting.
- **Message:** "Evil is the nature of mankind. Evil must be your only happiness" (66).

## Side characters
- **Goody Cloyse:** a "pious and exemplary dame" who taught Brown his catechism; greets the devil as an old friend and complains her broomstick was stolen. Later seen teaching a child.
- **The minister:** Salem's respected pastor; Brown hears him riding to the meeting. Next morning Brown shrinks from his blessing.
- **Deacon Gookin:** a church leader riding with the minister; later heard praying, and Brown thinks "What God doth the wizard pray to?"
- **Martha Carrier:** a real woman hanged in the 1692 witch trials; in the story the devil promised to make her "queen of hell."
- **Brown's father and mother (shapes):** at the meeting his dead father beckons him forward and a woman (his mother?) warns him back.
- **The "fiend worshippers":** the whole "good" town, plus people known for sin, together at the altar.

## Plot (detailed)
1. **Exposition:** at sunset Brown leaves Faith, who begs him to stay; he promises "this one night."
2. He feels guilty ("Poor little Faith!") but plans to "cling to her skirts" afterward.
3. **Rising action:** in the dark forest he meets the traveler, who says he's late ("Faith kept me back a while").
4. Brown wants to turn back, saying his family were good Christians; the traveler says he knew them well and lists their sins.
5. They see Goody Cloyse on the path; she recognizes the devil and goes off with his staff.
6. Brown sits on a stump and refuses to continue; the traveler leaves him a maple staff and vanishes.
7. Brown hears the minister and Deacon Gookin riding to the meeting.
8. He looks up for heaven, then hears voices in a black cloud, including Faith's. A **pink ribbon** falls: "My Faith is gone!"
9. Maddened, he rushes laughing through the forest toward the meeting.
10. **Climax:** at a fiery rock altar, the whole town gathers; the devil welcomes the converts, including Brown and Faith, and says evil is human nature.
11. Brown cries "Faith! Faith! look up to heaven, and resist the wicked one" and instantly finds himself alone in the cold, quiet forest.
12. **Falling action:** next morning he shrinks from the minister, Deacon Gookin, and Goody Cloyse, and passes Faith "without a greeting."
13. **Resolution:** dream or real? "Be it so if you will." Either way, he lives distrustful and dies in gloom: "they carved no hopeful verse upon his tombstone."

# Transcendentalism (In Depth)

## What it is
An American philosophical, spiritual, and literary movement (about **1830s–1850s**), centered in **Concord, Massachusetts**. It grew out of **Romanticism** and was influenced by German idealist philosophy, English Romantic poets, and Eastern religion.

**Key people:** **Ralph Waldo Emerson** (*Nature*, 1836; "Self-Reliance," 1841; "The Transcendentalist," 1842), **Henry David Thoreau** (*Walden*, 1854; "Civil Disobedience," 1849), and **Margaret Fuller** (editor of *The Dial*, the movement's journal; early feminist).

## Core beliefs
1. **Intuition over logic and the senses.** The deepest truths can't be proved by science or seen with your eyes; you "transcend" (go beyond) the senses and know them by intuition.
2. **The divine is in nature and in every person.** Emerson called it the **Over-Soul**: one shared spiritual reality connecting all people and nature. So nature is sacred, and studying it leads to truth.
3. **Self-reliance.** Trust yourself, not society, churches, or governments. "Trust thyself" (Emerson). Conformity is the enemy.
4. **Individual over institutions.** Organized religion, politics, and social rules corrupt people's pure inner goodness.
5. **People are basically good.** Unlike the Puritans (people are born sinful), Transcendentalists believed human nature is good and can reach the divine directly.
6. **Simplicity.** Thoreau lived simply at Walden Pond for two years to "live deliberately."
7. **Moral action.** Many opposed slavery and the Mexican-American War, and supported women's rights. Thoreau refused to pay taxes and went to jail ("Civil Disobedience").

## Emerson's "The Transcendentalist" (your handout)
- **Materialist:** "takes his departure from the external world, and esteems a man as one product of that." Values "Society, Government, social art, and luxury," big numbers and big things.
- **Idealist (= Transcendentalist):** "takes his departure from his consciousness, and reckons the world an appearance." Measures things by their "rank... in his consciousness," not their size.
- **"Mind is the only reality."** People and nature are "better or worse reflectors" of it. Nature, literature, and history are "subjective phenomena."
- The idealist respects labor, property, government, church, and charity **only as symbols** of deeper laws, not for themselves.
- **"His thought,—that is the Universe."** Everything flows outward from an "Unknown Centre" within himself.

## Transcendentalism vs. related ideas
| | Puritanism | Rationalism (Enlightenment) | Romanticism | Transcendentalism | Dark Romanticism (Poe, Hawthorne) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Human nature | Sinful | Reasonable | Emotional, individual | Good, divine within | Capable of evil and madness |
| Path to truth | Scripture, church | Logic, science | Emotion, imagination | Intuition, nature, self | Exploring sin, guilt, the mind's darkness |
| View of nature | Wild, dangerous | Orderly system | Beautiful, powerful | Sacred, reflects God | Mysterious, often threatening |
| Example | Salem elders (YGB) | Usher narrator's "logic" | Irving, Keats | Emerson, Thoreau | "Usher," "Young Goodman Brown" |

## How it connects to Unit 1
- **"Young Goodman Brown":** Hawthorne *pushes back* on Transcendentalism. Emerson says people are good inside; Hawthorne shows a whole town hiding evil ("virtue is a mask and all men are guilty," Intro p. 14). But it's also idealist: Brown's **mind** decides reality. Whether or not it was a dream, his belief ruins his life.
- **"The Fall of the House of Usher":** idealist in a dark way: the house reflects Roderick's mind; the inner world shapes the outer one. Poe's terror is "not of Germany but of the soul."
- **"Rip Van Winkle":** the **materialist** contrast: society and government change totally (King → Washington), but Rip's inner self doesn't. The outside world isn't what really defines him.
- **"The Eve of St. Agnes":** dream vs. reality; Madeline values her inner vision over the real world, an idealist idea.
- **"Baker's Bluejay Yarn":** Twain mocks big ideas with humor; the jay's obsession with the physical hole (filling it with "stuff") is a materialist joke. Your note connected it to the Gold Rush.
- **The American Dream:** Transcendentalists would say the real Dream is not wealth (materialist) but becoming your fullest self, close to Adams' "fuller" life "for everyone."

## Quick test facts
- Dates: 1830s–1850s · Place: Concord, MA · Leader: Emerson · Journal: *The Dial*
- Key word: **"transcend"** = go beyond (the senses / the physical)
- Emerson's two types: **materialist** (outside world) vs. **idealist** (mind) → Transcendentalists are idealists
- Famous lines: "Mind is the only reality" · "Trust thyself" · Thoreau: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately"
