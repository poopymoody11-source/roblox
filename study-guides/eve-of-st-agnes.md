# The Eve of St. Agnes — John Keats (1819)

**📝 On the Unit 1 exam (Tue 10/6)** · **Genre:** narrative poem, 42 stanzas · **Movement:** English Romanticism (with Gothic touches)
**Summary:** On a freezing St. Agnes' Eve, Madeline goes to bed early, hoping a legend will make her dream of her future husband. Porphyro, a young man from an enemy family, sneaks into her family's castle during a party. With the old nurse Angela's help, he hides in Madeline's room, then wakes her, and the two run away together into the storm.

---

## 🧠 Confusing Terms in Plain English
**Story/history words**
- **St. Agnes:** a 13-year-old Roman girl killed for her Christian faith (around 304 A.D.). She's the patron saint of young unmarried women.
- **St. Agnes' Eve:** January 20th, the night before her feast day. **The legend:** if a girl goes to bed without dinner, lies on her back, and doesn't look behind her, she'll **dream of her future husband** (lines 53–63).
- **Beadsman:** an old man paid to pray for other people's souls. He counts prayers on rosary beads.
- **Penance:** punishing yourself to make up for sins.
- **Feuding families:** families that hate each other, like the Montagues and Capulets in *Romeo and Juliet*. Porphyro's family and Madeline's family are enemies.

**Literature words**
- **Narrative poem:** a poem that tells a story.
- **Stanza:** a poem's version of a paragraph. This poem has 42 stanzas of 9 lines each.
- **Imagery:** descriptions you can see, hear, feel, smell, or taste.
- **Juxtaposition:** putting two opposite things side by side so the contrast stands out, like freezing cold vs. a warm room.
- **Simile:** a comparison using "like" or "as." "Like a mermaid in sea-weed."
- **Metaphor:** a comparison without "like" or "as." "Thou art my heaven."
- **Personification:** giving human traits to non-human things. Trumpets that "chide," or scold.
- **Allusion:** a reference to an outside story, person, or text, like Merlin.
- **Foreshadowing:** hints about what's coming later.
- **Frame:** opening and closing the story in a way that "frames" it. Here it's the cold old Beadsman at the start and at the end.

**Big-idea words**
- **Romanticism:** a movement (about 1800–1870) that valued feelings, imagination, nature, the supernatural, and the past (especially medieval times).
- **Gothic:** a darker, extreme form of Romanticism: old castles, ghosts, danger, a "pleasing sort of terror."

## Characters
- **Madeline:** a young, **pious** (very religious) noblewoman obsessed with the St. Agnes legend. She ignores every man at the party: "her heart was otherwhere" (71). She's dreamy and innocent, compared to "a splendid angel" (248). When she wakes and sees the real Porphyro, she's disappointed and scared: "How chang'd thou art! how pallid, chill, and drear!" (346). Then she runs away with him.
- **Porphyro:** a young man from the **enemy family**, "with heart on fire / For Madeline" (85–86). He's brave, because her family would kill him, but also **sneaky**: he hides in her closet and watches her undress. Angela calls him "A cruel man and impious" (156). Is he a romantic hero or a creep? That debate is a good exam point.
- **Angela:** the old nurse ("beldame"), "weak in body and in soul" (101), and the only person in the house who's kind to Porphyro. She warns him they'll kill him (109–120). She first refuses his plan, then gives in and hides him. She **dies** at the end (417–418). She's like Juliet's nurse in *Romeo and Juliet*.
- **The Beadsman:** a cold, old, praying man who **opens and closes** the poem. "Numb were the Beadsman's fingers" (7). At the end he dies "among his ashes cold" (420). He stands for age, death, and strict religion, the opposite of the young lovers.
- **The Baron, Hildebrand, and Lord Maurice:** Madeline's "blood-thirsty" family and their guests (110–117). They're dangerous, then drunk and asleep ("bloated wassailers," 385).

## Plot (by stanza)
1. **Exposition (1–4):** a freezing night. The Beadsman prays in the chapel among stone statues, while loud party music starts.
2. **(5–8):** the party. Madeline ignores the guests because she's thinking about the St. Agnes legend (stanza 6 explains it).
3. **Rising action (9–14):** Porphyro sneaks in. Angela warns him that the family will kill him, then tells him Madeline's plan.
4. **(15–20):** Porphyro gets a "stratagem" (sneaky plan): hide in Madeline's room. Angela is horrified, but he swears he won't harm her, and she agrees and hides him in the closet.
5. **(21–27):** Madeline comes in, prays in the moonlight, undresses, and falls asleep. Porphyro watches from the closet.
6. **(28–32):** he sets out a feast of exotic foods, then plays her lute ("La belle dame sans mercy").
7. **Climax (33–36):** Madeline wakes, but she's confused between her dream-Porphyro and the real one. He "melted" into her dream (356). "This is no dream, my bride, my Madeline!" (363).
8. **Falling action (37–40):** he promises he's "no rude infidel" and has "a home for thee" (390). They sneak past the drunk porter and the bloodhound.
9. **Resolution (41–42):** "they are gone: ay, ages long ago" (412). The Baron has nightmares, Angela dies, and the Beadsman dies cold.

## Setting & Mood
A medieval castle on a **freezing, stormy St. Agnes' Eve** (January 20th), long ago.
*(Teacher tip from your notes: quote the full phrase, not just one adjective.)*
- "St. Agnes' Eve—Ah, bitter chill it was!" (3) and "The hare limp'd trembling through the frozen grass" (5): **cold, harsh, and lonely**. Even the animals are suffering.
- "The silver, snarling trumpets 'gan to chide" (36): **loud and aggressive**. The party is noisy and almost threatening.
- "He found him in a little moonlight room, / Pale, lattic'd, chill, and silent as a tomb" (125–126): **eerie and deathly**, the Gothic side of the poem.
- "candied apple, quince, and plum, and gourd; / With jellies soother than the creamy curd" (295–296): **warm, rich, and dreamy**, a luxurious mood that contrasts with the cold outside.
- "They glide, like phantoms, into the wide hall" (402): **ghostly and mysterious**. The lovers seem to vanish like ghosts.

## Author: John Keats (1795–1821)
- An **English Romantic poet** who died of **tuberculosis at just 25**. He trained as a medical student and nursed his brother Tom, who died of the same disease in 1818.
- He wrote this poem in early 1819 while **in love with Fanny Brawne** but too poor and too sick to marry her. A poem about two lovers escaping every obstacle to be together reflects that longing.
- Being so close to death may explain why **cold, aging, and dying** frame the poem (the Beadsman and Angela), with young love burning in the middle.

## Time Period
- **1819, the height of English Romanticism** (the same year as "Rip Van Winkle"). Romantics loved the **medieval past, legends, the supernatural, and intense emotion**, and this poem has all four.
- It's English, but it's in Unit 1 to show what **Romanticism** looks like, so you can spot the same traits in American writers like Irving and Poe.

## Key Literary Devices
| Device | Example (line) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Imagery** | "his frosted breath, / Like pious incense" (8–9) | You can *see* the cold, and it connects cold to prayer. |
| **Juxtaposition** | Freezing chapel and old Beadsman vs. the warm feast and young lovers | Old vs. young, cold vs. warm, death vs. love |
| **Personification** | "Music's golden tongue" (24); trumpets "'gan to chide" (36) | The party feels alive and loud. |
| **Simile** | "As though a rose should shut, and be a bud again" (270) | Sleep makes Madeline innocent and closed off, like a rosebud. |
| **Metaphor** | "Love's fev'rous citadel" (94); "Thou art my heaven" (308) | Love is a fortress under attack, and love is like religion. |
| **Allusion** | Merlin (190); "La belle dame sans mercy" (325); St. Agnes | Connects the poem to medieval legends and tragic love stories. |
| **Foreshadowing** | "already had his deathbell rung" (26) | The Beadsman's death is set up from the start. |
| **Frame** | Beadsman at the opening (stanzas 1–3) and the ending (42) | Cold and death surround the warm love story. |

## Themes
- **Dream vs. reality:** "This is no dream, my bride, my Madeline!" (363). Madeline's dream is perfect, and the real Porphyro is "pallid, chill." Real life is never as perfect as the dream.
- **Young love vs. hate:** the lovers escape a family that's a "whole blood-thirsty race" (110). It's like *Romeo and Juliet*, but they get away.
- **Warmth and life vs. cold and death:** the feast and the love are surrounded by freezing cold, the dying Angela, and the Beadsman "among his ashes cold" (420).
- **Religion vs. desire:** Madeline prays "for heaven's grace" (244) while Porphyro hides and watches her. Holy and earthly love clash.

## Essential Question: How can we critically interpret the American Dream?
James Truslow Adams' Dream is a better life for anyone, **"regardless of the circumstances of birth."** Porphyro and Madeline are trapped by the families they were born into, but they run off to make their own life: "I have a home for thee" (390). **Unit 1 (literature as a cultural artifact):** the poem is a perfect sample of Romanticism (medieval setting, legend, emotion, and the supernatural), the movement that shaped American writers like Irving and Poe.

## Class Questions (from the unit plan)
- **What is the Eve of St. Agnes?** January 20th, when girls who follow the rituals dream of their future husbands (53–63). St. Agnes was a young martyr and the patron saint of virgins.
- **What's the story, and Keats' message?** Porphyro sneaks in, wakes Madeline, and they escape (see the plot section). **Message:** love is worth risking everything, but dreams are more perfect than reality, and youth and love are brief, surrounded by age and death.
- **How do the devices help tell the story?** Cold imagery makes the warm love scenes stand out. Allusions (Merlin, "La belle dame") make it feel like an old legend. The Beadsman frame reminds us that everyone dies.
- **Connection to "Usher" / "The Haunted Palace" / Ethelred:**
  - **Your idea: the "fall" of the house.** In "Usher," the last Ushers (Roderick and Madeline) die and the house literally collapses. Here, the castle doesn't fall physically, but its **future** walks out the door. Madeline (the Baron's daughter) runs off with the enemy's son, leaving behind only drunk guests, a Baron who "dreamt of many a woe" (414), a dead nurse (418), and a dead Beadsman (420). The family line is symbolically "dead."
  - **Fun detail:** both heroines are named **Madeline**, and both are tied to a house's end.
  - **Also similar:** a medieval, supernatural, Gothic setting; a story within a story (the St. Agnes legend / "The Haunted Palace" and "The Mad Tryst"); and a cold, tomb-like house ("silent as a tomb," 126).
  - **How to word it:** the poem never says Madeline is the *last* heir, so phrase it as an interpretation: "Madeline's escape **can be read as** the symbolic fall of the Baron's house." 

## Practice Questions
1. **What does Madeline need to do on St. Agnes' Eve?** Skip dinner, lie on her back, and not look behind her, so she'll dream of her future husband.
2. **Why is Porphyro in danger?** His family is the enemy of Madeline's family, which would kill him (stanzas 10–11).
3. **Who frames the poem, and why?** The Beadsman, at the beginning and the end. His coldness and death surround the warm love story.
4. **Pivotal:** *To what extent does Keats present Porphyro as a romantic hero, and why is this significant?* He's brave and devoted ("heart on fire," 85). But he also uses a "stratagem," hides, and watches Madeline undress, and Angela calls him "cruel" and "impious" (156). Keats leaves it **ambiguous**, which makes readers question whether romantic dreams hide uncomfortable realities.

## Connections
- **"Rip Van Winkle":** both are about **sleep and dreams**, and both are from **1819**. Rip wakes to a changed world. Madeline wakes to a "changed" Porphyro. Both blur dream and reality, and both have a legend explaining the magic (Hudson's ghosts / St. Agnes).
- **Romanticism packet:** medieval setting, legend, emotion, and the supernatural all match the "characteristic attitudes" you bracketed as "Key points."
- **"The Fall of the House of Usher":** both houses "fall" at the end, Usher literally and the Baron's house symbolically, when its daughter leaves with the enemy (your idea, explained under Class Questions).
- **"The Custom-House" (Unit 2):** both are framed by **old men** (the Beadsman / the old Inspector and General) who represent age and decay.
