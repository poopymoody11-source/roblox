# Rip Van Winkle — Washington Irving (1819)

**Genre:** short story / tall tale, frame narrative · **Movement:** American Romanticism
**Summary:** Lazy, likable Rip escapes his nagging wife into the Catskills, drinks with the ghosts of Henry Hudson's crew, and sleeps **20 years**. He wakes to find his wife dead, his daughter grown, and King George replaced by George Washington. He slept through the Revolution.

---

## Characters
- **Rip:** kind to everyone, but has "an insuperable aversion to all kinds of profitable labor" (p. 35). **Static**: just as idle at the end, but now nobody cares.
- **Dame Van Winkle:** nagging wife ("termagant"). **Flat**, and a **foil** to Rip. Stands for strict rule, like England. Dies in a rage.
- **Wolf:** Rip's dog and "fellow-sufferer in persecution."
- **Nicholas Vedder:** innkeeper and village patriarch; dead by the time Rip returns.
- **Hudson's ghost crew:** silent Dutchmen playing ninepins (the thunder); the source of the magic liquor.
- **Judith:** Rip's grown daughter, who takes him in. **Rip Jr.** is just as lazy as his father.
- **Man in the cocked hat:** loud new politician who calls Rip "A tory! a spy!" (p. 46). Satire.
- **Diedrich Knickerbocker:** fake narrator from the Preface (Irving's hoax persona).

## Plot
1. **Preface:** the story was "found among the papers of the late Diedrich Knickerbocker." It's a frame that makes it look like real history.
2. **Exposition:** the magical mountains; lazy Rip and his ruined farm; his nagging wife.
3. **Rising action:** Rip flees to the woods, a stranger calls his name, he carries a keg up to the ghosts playing ninepins, and he secretly drinks.
4. **Climax:** he falls asleep.
5. **Falling action:** he wakes with a rusty gun and a foot-long beard. The village is unrecognizable: the inn is the "Union Hotel" and the King's sign now says "GENERAL WASHINGTON." He's nearly mobbed as a Tory and doubts who he is ("I'm not myself — I'm somebody else," p. 47).
6. **Resolution:** Judith recognizes him, and old Peter Vanderdonk confirms the Hudson legend. Rip happily tells his tale at the inn forever.

## Setting & Mood
A Dutch village under the Kaatskill (Catskill) Mountains, NY, before and after the Revolution.
- "these **fairy mountains**" / "magical hues and shapes" (p. 33): **dreamy, magical**, and it foreshadows the supernatural.
- "a deep mountain glen, wild, lonely, and shagged" (p. 39): **eerie and isolated**.
- "busy, bustling, disputatious... instead of the accustomed... drowsy tranquillity" (p. 45): **noisy and confusing**, the new America.

## Author: Washington Irving (1783–1859)
- Born as the Revolution ended and **named after George Washington**. The story is about that same transition.
- Teen trips through the **Catskills and Dutch ghost stories** became the setting and the legend.
- Loved **pseudonyms and hoaxes**: the Knickerbocker "missing historian" ads became the story's frame.
- Went **bankrupt** in England and wrote Rip **overnight** there. Lived in Europe for 17 years, so, like Rip, he came home to a changed America.
- Read **German/Dutch folk tales** (Rip is adapted from one).
- First American to live off writing alone. Wrote to **entertain, not preach**. **Influenced** Hawthorne, Poe, Melville, and Longfellow.
- Cultural impact: "Gotham," "Knickerbocker" (the Knicks), "the almighty dollar," and the Santa Claus image.

## Time Period
- Written in **1819**, when the U.S. was young and trying to prove it had its own literature.
- **Romanticism** valued nature, emotion, imagination, **folklore and national origins**, and the supernatural, against Enlightenment reason.
- "Federal or Democrat?" refers to the early party fights. Irving **satirizes** how loud and confusing the new politics felt to ordinary people.

## Key Literary Devices
| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Frame / persona** | Knickerbocker preface (p. 31) | Fake "history," adds **ambiguity** |
| **Imagery + personification** | Mountains "lording it over the surrounding country" (p. 33) | Nature feels alive and magical (Romanticism) |
| **Foreshadowing** | "fairy mountains"; ninepins sound like "distant thunder" | Hints at the supernatural |
| **Symbol** | Inn sign: King George → "GENERAL WASHINGTON" | Government changed, people didn't |
| **Metaphor** | Marriage as "petticoat government," "despotism," "yoke" (p. 50) | Links Rip's escape to America's escape from England |
| **Satire / irony** | Apolitical Rip accused of being a Tory spy | Mocks self-important new politics |
| **Aphorism** | "a sharp tongue is the only edged tool that grows keener with constant use" (p. 37) | Witty jab at his wife |
| **Tall tale** | Sleeping 20 years | Exaggerated, humorous, unbelievable |

## Themes
- **Change vs. staying the same:** the nation transforms, but Rip doesn't.
- **Freedom:** Rip is "freed" from his wife the way America was freed from England.
- **Identity:** without his past, Rip doesn't know who he is.
- **Nature and the supernatural** (Romanticism).

## Essential Question: How can we critically interpret the American Dream?
Rip **flips** the work-hard-get-rich Dream. He avoids "profitable labor," loses his farm, and still ends up happy and respected. Irving suggests the busy, money-driven new America isn't the only path to a good life. **Unit 1 (literature as cultural artifact):** the story preserves Dutch folklore and the moment America went from colony to nation.

## Practice Questions
1. **What did Rip sleep through?** The American Revolution (20 years).
2. **Why the Knickerbocker preface?** It's a frame that makes a tall tale seem like true history, continuing Irving's hoax.
3. **Three Romantic traits?** Magical nature, the supernatural ghosts, and folklore and national origins.
4. **Pivotal:** *In what ways does Irving link Rip's escape from his wife to America's escape from England, and why is this significant?* He calls his marriage "petticoat government" and "despotism" (p. 50), political language. Rip only cares about *personal* freedom, so Irving satirizes the Revolution's grand claims.

## Connections
- **"The Custom-House":** both narrators "find old papers" to make fiction seem real, and both gently satirize sleepy old institutions.
- **"Young Goodman Brown":** a man goes into the woods, has a supernatural encounter, and comes home changed. Rip comes home happy; Brown comes home gloomy.
