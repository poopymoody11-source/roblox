# Class unit plan: relevant info (from the teacher's Reading & Literature Unit Plan)

## Unit 1 exam (Tue 10/6): Great American Short Stories
**Fair game:**
- All stories and nonfiction from *Great American Short Stories* (GrAmSS), including its **Introduction**
- "The Eve of St. Agnes" (Keats)
- The Washington Irving / Romanticism packet
- The **Emerson handout**

- Handouts and notes

**Not on the Unit 1 exam:**
- "The Custom House" and *The Scarlet Letter*. They're on the **Unit 2** exam. "Young Goodman Brown" is the only Hawthorne work on Unit 1.

When making a guide, add a one-line **"On which exam?"** note at the top.

## Key class definitions
- **American Dream:** the teacher uses the **original definition by James Truslow Adams** (*The Epic of America*, 1931): a land where life should be better, richer, and fuller for everyone, with opportunity based on ability or achievement, regardless of the class or circumstances of birth. It's not just about money; it's a dream of social order where each person can reach their fullest potential. Class asks: what is it, based on his diction? What is it *not*? How has it evolved?
- **Setting:** the time and place of a story.
- **Characterization:** how the author breathes life into the people in the story.
- **Mood:** the feeling or atmosphere the author creates.

## How the teacher analyzes (mirror this in guides)
- **Imagery → setting → mood, 3 steps:** (1) identify the evidence (imagery describing the setting), (2) label the mood it creates, and (3) circle the key words that create that feeling.
- **Characterization → central idea / author's purpose.**
- **Rhetorical analysis (AP style):** how does the author's language (literary/rhetorical devices) convey the message? Include **ethos, logos, and pathos**.
- **Author's life → choices** in characters, plot, and story.
- **Allusion / stories within stories:** why does the author embed other works?
- **Pivotal questions** use these openers: *To what extent… / In what ways… / For what reasons… / How is/does… and why is it significant?* They're open-ended, never recall.
- **Paragraph answers:** one cohesive response with evidence for every part, using transitions (the class's "Effective Paragraph" structure). Vocab definitions should fit the **context** (OED style).

## Teacher's discussion questions by text (answer these in each guide's "Class Questions" section)

### "Rip Van Winkle"
*Aim: How does Irving use characterization to develop a central idea / his purpose?*
- How does Irving use **imagery** to introduce the setting? (pp. 33–34)
- 1a. How does the characterization of Rip, Wolf, and Dame establish Irving's notions of males and females? (p. 36)
- 1b. To what extent does Irving's language suggest he favors one sex over another? (pp. 35–36)
- 2a. How does Irving use Dame and Rip to appeal to **pathos**?
- 2b. To what extent do you favor one character over the other?
- 3. Considering the time period (George III → Washington), how might Rip's slumber let Irving comment on the times? (pp. 44–46)
- Reflect: how did Irving's own life influence his characters, plot, and story?
- **Class vocab list:** Diedrich Knickerbocker, burghers (31), scrupulous (32), latticed, surmounted, weathercocks, obsequious, conciliating, termagant (34), insuperable, aversion, assiduity, dale, pestilent, patrimonial, urchin (35), galli-gaskins (36), rubicund, junto, adherents (37), approbation, virago (38), jerkin (39), alacrity (40), smote, flagons (41), roysterers (42), connubial (44), akimbo (46)
- **Packet vocab:** Romanticism, historiography, Classicism, Neoclassicism, Enlightenment, Rationalism, Transcendental, exaltation, predilection, occult, satanic

### "The Eve of St. Agnes" (Keats)
*Aims: researching vocab and identifying devices; reading for comprehension, not completion.*
- What is the Eve of St. Agnes, and who was St. Agnes? (Jan. 20 legend: a girl who performs rituals will dream of her future husband.)
- What is the story of the poem, and how do you know? What is Keats' message?
- Which devices appear in each stanza, and how do they help convey the story?
- Connection: how does it relate to "The Haunted Palace" and Ethelred in Usher?

### "The Fall of the House of Usher" (Poe)
*Aims: How does Poe's language convey his message? (AP rhetorical analysis); why does Poe embed other works?*
- How does Poe use imagery to introduce the setting? (opening p. 69: "dull, dark, and soundless day," "melancholy House of Usher," "insufferable gloom," "vacant eye-like windows," "black and lurid tarn")
- Characterize Roderick (70–71, 74), Madeline (77, 90), and the house through imagery (72–73, 91). Find the overall pattern in word choice and feeling.
- What is Poe's message or purpose? How does his language build ethos, logos, and pathos?
- "The Haunted Palace" (79–81): the story of each stanza, and how it mirrors Roderick's mind and the house (a bright palace = a sane mind; "evil things" invade; "red-litten windows" = madness)
- Ethelred / "The Mad Tryst" (87–89): how its sounds match Madeline escaping her tomb
- **Poem vocab:** seraph, pinion, ramparts, Porphyrogene, discordant, throng, lute

### "Baker's Bluejay Yarn" (Twain)
- Context research: what was happening in the world **1835–1880**? (Include the historical context in the guide.)
