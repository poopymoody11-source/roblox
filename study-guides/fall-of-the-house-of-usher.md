# The Fall of the House of Usher — Edgar Allan Poe (1839)

**📝 On the Unit 1 exam (Tue 10/6)** · **Genre:** short story, Gothic fiction · **Movement:** American Romanticism (dark/Gothic side) · **Pages:** GrAmSS 69–91 (complete)
**Summary:** An unnamed narrator rides to the gloomy House of Usher after his sick childhood friend Roderick begs him to visit. Roderick's twin sister Madeline seems to die, and the two men seal her in a vault under the house. She was buried alive. During a storm she breaks out, falls dead on Roderick (who dies of terror), and the narrator flees as the house splits apart and sinks into the dark lake.

---

## 🧠 Confusing Terms in Plain English
**Story words**
- **Narrator:** the "I" telling the story. We never learn his name.
- **Tarn:** a small, dark mountain lake. The house sits right next to it.
- **"House of Usher":** means **both** the building **and** the family (p. 71). When the house falls, the family ends too. That's a double meaning.
- **Catalepsy / cataleptic:** a medical condition where the body goes stiff and looks dead. That's why Madeline gets buried alive.
- **Hypochondriac:** someone obsessed with being sick. Roderick is called this.
- **Donjon / vault:** an underground dungeon or tomb room. Madeline is placed there (p. 83).
- **Premature burial:** being buried while still alive. Poe's favorite nightmare.

**Literature words**
- **Gothic fiction:** an extreme, dark form of Romanticism with creepy old buildings, madness, death, the supernatural, and "a pleasing sort of terror." *Your definition: Romanticism "infused with the horror genre to evoke a specific reaction within the reader."* ✔
- **Mood:** the feeling or atmosphere the author creates (your teacher's MOT definition).
- **Imagery:** descriptions you can see, hear, or feel.
- **Personification:** giving human traits to non-human things. The house has "eye-like windows."
- **Symbol:** an object that stands for a bigger idea. The crack in the house stands for the family breaking.
- **Foreshadowing:** hints about what's coming. The crack hints that the house will fall.
- **Allusion:** a reference to an outside text. Your notes say "reference to outside text." ✔
- **Story within a story:** a poem or tale placed inside the main story ("The Haunted Palace," "The Mad Trist").
- **Unreliable narrator:** a narrator we can't fully trust. He admits his "superstition" is growing.
- **Ethos / logos / pathos:** convincing someone through credibility, logic, or emotion.

## Characters
- **The Narrator:** Roderick's boyhood friend who comes because of the desperate letter. He's logical and tries to explain things away ("merely electrical phenomena," p. 86), but slowly gets infected by the fear: "It was no wonder that his condition terrified—that it infected me" (p. 84). **Dynamic**: calm to terrified.
- **Roderick Usher:** the last male Usher. He's pale and sickly, with a "cadaverousness of complexion" and a "ghastly pallor" (p. 74). His senses are painfully sharp: he can only eat bland food, and light and flower smells torture him (p. 75). He's an artist and musician who's terrified of fear itself: "I shall perish... in this deplorable folly" (p. 75). He hears Madeline in the tomb for days but stays silent: "We have put her living in the tomb!" (p. 89). He dies of terror when she appears. **Round**, and he's a **double** of the house.
- **Lady Madeline Usher:** Roderick's **twin**, his "sole companion" and "last and only relative on earth" (p. 76). She barely appears and never speaks. She has a mystery disease with "a settled apathy, a gradual wasting away" (p. 77). After she's buried alive she returns: "There was blood upon her white robes" (p. 90). She's **flat**, more like a ghost or symbol than a person.
- **The House:** basically a character too. It's personified, decaying, and dies with the family. *(Your group's assignment: groups 3 and 4.)*
- **Minor:** the valet, and the family physician with "an expression of low cunning and perplexity" (p. 73).

## Plot
1. **Exposition (pp. 69–71):** on a "dull, dark, and soundless day," the narrator arrives at the gloomy house. A letter from Roderick begged him to come because Roderick is sick. The Usher family line has only ever passed straight down from parent to child, with no branches.
2. **(pp. 72–73):** he notices a barely visible crack running from roof to tarn and enters the house through dark passages.
3. **Rising action (pp. 74–77):** Roderick looks horribly changed and explains his "family evil." Madeline drifts past and disappears. She takes to her bed that night.
4. **(pp. 77–81):** the narrator and Roderick paint, read, and play music to pass the days. Roderick sings "The Haunted Palace" and believes the house's stones are *alive* ("sentience," p. 81).
5. **(pp. 82–83):** Roderick announces Madeline is "no more." They put her in a vault under the house for two weeks. The narrator learns they were **twins**, and notices a faint blush on her cheeks and a lingering smile. That's a clue she isn't dead.
6. **(pp. 84–85):** Roderick roams the house terrified. On the 7th or 8th night a storm rages, and Roderick throws open the window.
7. **Climax (pp. 86–90):** the narrator reads "The Mad Trist" aloud. Each sound in the story (cracking wood, a dragon's shriek, a clanging shield) is **echoed by real sounds in the house**. Roderick confesses she was buried alive, and Madeline appears at the door, bloody, and falls on him. Both die.
8. **Resolution (pp. 90–91):** the narrator flees. The crack widens under a blood-red moon, the house splits apart, and the tarn swallows "the fragments of the House of Usher."

## Setting & Mood
The isolated Usher mansion beside a black tarn, in autumn, cut off from everyone.
*(Teacher tip: quote the full phrase, not one word.)*
- "a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens" (p. 69): **depressing and heavy**. *Your note: "gives you an anxious feeling."* ✔
- "the bleak walls—upon the vacant eye-like windows" (p. 69): **creepy and watched**. The house seems to stare like a skull.
- "an atmosphere which had no affinity with the air of heaven" (p. 72): **unnatural and evil**, the opposite of heaven.
- "Minute fungi overspread the whole exterior, hanging in a fine tangled webwork from the eaves" (p. 72): **rotting and decayed**.
- "I felt that I breathed an atmosphere of sorrow. An air of stern, deep, and irredeemable gloom hung over and pervaded all" (p. 73): **hopeless gloom** inside too.
- "the deep and dank tarn at my feet closed sullenly and silently over the fragments" (p. 91): **final and empty**. Everything is gone.

## Author: Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849)
- **Orphaned** at 2 when his mother died of tuberculosis. He was raised by the Allans and later cut off by his foster father. He lived much of his life in **poverty**.
- His young wife Virginia (also his cousin) slowly died of tuberculosis, and he struggled with **alcohol and depression**. He died mysteriously at 40.
- **Life → story:** watching women he loved waste away mirrors Madeline's "gradual wasting away." His depression mirrors Roderick's "gloom" and fear of madness. And his fear of death and decay is in every line.
- **Irving connection:** Poe sent Irving his stories ("William Wilson" and "The Fall of the House of Usher") for advice (Irving packet p. 5). Irving encouraged him (p. 3).

## Time Period
- **1839**, in American Romanticism. Poe took the **Gothic** (castles, horror, madness) from England and Germany and made it famous in America.
- It's an "idealist" text in Emerson's sense, since it's all inside the mind. Many readers see the house as Roderick's **mind** falling apart.
- It's set in no real place or time. Poe wanted the effect on the reader's emotions, not a lesson. Like Irving, he wrote to create an effect, not to preach.

## Key Literary Devices
| Device | Example (page) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Imagery** | "Minute fungi overspread the whole exterior" (72) | You can see the rot and decay. |
| **Personification** | "vacant eye-like windows" (69) | The house is alive, like a skull or face. |
| **Symbol** | The **fissure** (crack) "in a zigzag direction" (72, 90) | The family and Roderick's mind are splitting. |
| **Foreshadowing** | the crack (72); Madeline's "faint blush" and smile in the coffin (83) | Hints the house will fall and she's alive. |
| **Double meaning / pun** | "House of Usher" = family **and** mansion (71) | When one falls, so does the other. |
| **Allusion / story within story** | "The Haunted Palace" (79–81); "The Mad Trist" (86–89) | They mirror Roderick's mind and Madeline's escape. |
| **Simile** | "like the voice of a thousand waters" (91) | The roar of the house collapsing. |
| **Pathos** | "We have put her living in the tomb!" (89) | Horror and pity hit the reader. |

## Themes
- **Madness and fear:** "I shall perish... I must perish in this deplorable folly" (p. 75). Fear itself destroys Roderick.
- **Decay and death of a family:** the family "lay in the direct line of descent" (p. 71). No branches, so when the twins die, the line is gone.
- **The house as the mind / identity:** Roderick believes the stones are alive and "had molded the destinies of his family" (p. 81). The man and the house are one.
- **Being trapped / buried alive:** Madeline sealed in the "region of horror" (p. 83). Roderick is trapped too, since he hasn't left the house in years.

## Essential Question: How can we critically interpret the American Dream?
Adams' Dream says your life shouldn't be limited by **"the circumstances of birth."** The Ushers are the opposite: completely **trapped by their birth**. Their "family evil" (p. 75), their isolated estate, and their single line of inheritance doom them. Poe shows a dark flip side of the Dream, where inherited wealth and old family names lead to decay, not opportunity. **Unit 1 (cultural artifact):** it's a perfect sample of American Gothic Romanticism.

## Class Questions (from the unit plan)
- **How does Poe use imagery to introduce the setting? (p. 69)** Words like "dull, dark, and soundless," "oppressively low," "insufferable gloom," and "vacant eye-like windows" create a **depressing, anxious, dread-filled** mood before anything even happens.
- **Characterize Roderick (70–71, 74):** sickly ("cadaverousness," "ghastly pallor"), oversensitive, swinging between "vivacious" and "sullen," and terrified of fear itself. His body is falling apart like the house.
- **Characterize Madeline (77, 90):** silent and ghostly, "wasting away," then horrifyingly alive, "blood upon her white robes." She's the family's buried secret coming back.
- **The house through imagery (72–73, 91):** fungi, a zigzag crack, dark draperies, an "atmosphere of sorrow," and in the end it collapses. **Pattern:** every image is **decay, darkness, and death**.
- **Poe's message/purpose:** fear, isolation, and a family turned inward destroy the mind, and the mind and its "house" fall together. **Ethos:** the narrator acts calm and logical, so we trust him. **Logos:** he tries to explain things scientifically ("electrical phenomena"). **Pathos:** the terror of premature burial.
- **Why allude to other works?** They **mirror and predict** the main story (see below). Even if you don't know the originals, they add layers and build suspense.

## 🏰 Stories within the Story

### "The Haunted Palace" (pp. 79–81): Roderick's song
| Stanza | What happens | What it mirrors |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | A radiant palace stands in the greenest valley, guarded by angels, in "the monarch Thought's dominion" | Roderick's **healthy mind** (and the house in better days) |
| **II** | Yellow, golden banners float on the roof | His **hair**, and the family's glory "in the olden time long ago" |
| **III** | Wanderers see spirits dancing through "two luminous windows" around King Porphyrogene | His **eyes** (windows) and his orderly **thoughts** (spirits) around his reason (the king) |
| **IV** | Through a pearl-and-ruby door, Echoes sing "the wit and wisdom of their king" | His **mouth** (red lips, white teeth) and his **intelligent words** |
| **V** | "But evil things, in robes of sorrow, / Assailed the monarch's high estate" | **Madness and the family disease** attack his mind |
| **VI** | Through "red-litten windows," vast forms move to "a discordant melody," and a "hideous throng rush out forever, / And laugh—but smile no more" | His **bloodshot eyes**, chaotic thoughts, and **insane laughter**. The palace is now haunted |

**Big idea:** the palace = Roderick's head and mind, which is also the House of Usher. The narrator says it showed Roderick's awareness "of the tottering of his lofty reason upon her throne" (p. 79). It **foreshadows** both his madness and the fall of the house.

### "The Mad Trist" by Sir Launcelot Canning (pp. 86–89): the Ethelred story
| In the story Ethelred... | ...and at the same moment in the house |
|---|---|
| Smashes the hermit's wooden door: "cracked, and ripped, and tore" (87) | A "cracking and ripping sound" echoes from deep in the mansion (87) |
| Kills the dragon, which gives "a shriek so horrid and harsh" (88) | A "harsh, protracted... screaming or grating sound" (88) |
| The brass shield falls with "a mighty great and terrible ringing sound" (89) | A "hollow, metallic, and clangorous" reverberation (89) |

**What it really is:** Roderick explains: "the breaking of the hermit's door, and the death-cry of the dragon, and the clangor of the shield!—say, rather, the **rending of her coffin**, and the **grating of the iron hinges of her prison**, and her **struggles within the coppered archway of the vault**!" (pp. 89–90). Madeline is breaking out **at the exact same time** as the story. Poe uses it to build **suspense** and make the horror feel fated.

## Connections
- **"The Eve of St. Agnes":** *your idea:* both "great houses" end. Usher literally falls into the tarn, and the Baron's house loses its heir when Madeline runs away. Both have a **Madeline**, a deathlike sleep, a storm, and a Gothic castle.
- **"Rip Van Winkle":** Rip sleeps 20 years and wakes up. Madeline is "dead" but wakes in the tomb. Both blur sleep, death, and reality, and both use a legend or inner story (Hudson's crew / "The Mad Trist"). Irving mentored Poe.
- **Emerson (your notes):** an **idealist** story, since the horror lives in the mind. Poe even says Roderick "painted an idea" (p. 78).
- **"The Custom-House" (Unit 2):** both narrators enter old, decaying buildings full of old men and ghosts of the past.
