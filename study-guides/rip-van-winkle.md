## 1. Quick overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Washington Irving (1783–1859) |
| **Published** | 1819, in *The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.* |
| **Genre** | Short story / folk tale / tall tale, with a frame narrative |
| **Movement** | American **Romanticism** (early 1800s–1870s) |
| **Setting** | A Dutch village at the foot of the Kaatskill (Catskill) Mountains, New York, before and after the American Revolution |

**Summary:** Rip Van Winkle is a lazy but well-liked villager who avoids work and his nagging wife. He wanders into the mountains, drinks liquor with a strange crew of ghostly Dutchmen playing ninepins, and falls asleep for **20 years**. He wakes to find his wife dead, his daughter grown, and King George III replaced by George Washington, because the American Revolution happened while he slept.

## 3. Plot events (in order)

**Frame:** a **Preface** says the story was found in the papers of the dead historian Diedrich Knickerbocker, and a short poem epigraph comes before the story. This frame makes the tall tale *pretend* to be true history.

**Exposition**
1. Description of the Kaatskill Mountains and the old Dutch village (p. 33).
2. Rip is introduced: kind to children and neighbors, but refuses to work on his own farm. His farm falls apart (pp. 34–35).
3. Dame Van Winkle nags him constantly. He escapes to the inn's "club" with Nicholas Vedder (pp. 36–37).

**Rising action**

4. Dame Van Winkle even breaks up the inn club, so Rip escapes to the woods with his gun and Wolf (p. 38).
5. On a fall day he hears a voice calling "Rip Van Winkle!" and meets a strange old man carrying a keg (p. 39).
6. He helps carry the keg up to an amphitheater where odd Dutchmen silently play ninepins. The bowling sounds like thunder (pp. 40–41).
7. Rip secretly drinks the liquor again and again (p. 41).

**Climax**

8. Rip falls into a deep sleep (p. 41).

**Falling action**

9. He wakes up. His gun is rusty, Wolf is gone, and his beard is a foot long. He thinks he slept one night (p. 42).
10. In the village, no one knows him. His house is in ruins, and the inn is now the "Union Hotel." King George's portrait has become "GENERAL WASHINGTON" (pp. 44–45).
11. Political arguments: "Federal or Democrat?" He declares himself "a loyal subject of the King" and is nearly mobbed as a **Tory** (pp. 46–47).
12. He learns his friends are dead or changed. He sees his son and doubts his own identity: "I'm not myself — I'm somebody else" (p. 47).
13. His daughter Judith recognizes him. He learns his wife died from bursting a blood vessel in a rage (p. 48).

**Resolution**

14. Old Peter Vanderdonk explains that Hendrick Hudson's crew haunts the mountains every 20 years (p. 49).
15. Rip lives with his daughter, sits on the inn bench, and tells his story to every stranger. Henpecked husbands wish for a drink from Rip's flagon (pp. 50–51).

## 4. Setting & mood

**Place:** a small Dutch village on the Hudson River at the base of the Kaatskill (Catskill) Mountains, New York.
**Time:** about 20 years **before** the Revolution (British colony, King George III) and then **after** it (the new United States).

**Mood:** dreamy, magical, peaceful, and nostalgic at the start; eerie and mysterious in the mountains; confusing, noisy, and unsettling after he returns; comic throughout.

| Quote | How it creates mood |
|---|---|
| "Every change of season, every change of weather, indeed, every hour of the day produces some change in the magical hues and shapes of these mountains" (p. 33) | The mountains feel **alive and magical**, which foreshadows the supernatural event. |
| "these fairy mountains" (p. 33) | One word turns a real place into a **fairy-tale** setting. |
| "a deep mountain glen, wild, lonely, and shagged" (p. 39) | Creates an **isolated, eerie** mood just before Rip meets the ghosts. |
| "the most melancholy party of pleasure he had ever witnessed" (p. 41) | **Strange, unsettling** mood: a "party" with no fun or sound. |
| "Instead of the great tree that used to shelter the quiet little Dutch inn of yore, there now was reared a tall naked pole" (p. 44) | The peaceful past has been replaced by something bare and harsh, giving a **lost, disoriented** mood. |
| "a busy, bustling, disputatious tone about it, instead of the accustomed phlegm and drowsy tranquillity" (p. 45) | Shows the new **noisy, anxious** America next to the old calm. |

## 5. Author: Washington Irving (1783–1859)

**Key facts from your packet (pp. 3–6):**
- Born **April 3, 1783**, in Manhattan, the same week New Yorkers learned the British ceasefire ended the Revolution. He was **named after George Washington** and met him at age six.
- An "uninterested student" who snuck out to see plays. As a teen, he traveled up the **Hudson** and through the **Catskills**, where he heard **Dutch customs and ghost stories**. He later wrote: "the Kaatskill Mountains had the most witching effect on my boyish imagination."
- Wrote under **pseudonyms**: Jonathan Oldstyle, Geoffrey Crayon, and **Diedrich Knickerbocker**.
- Ran a famous **hoax** in 1809: he put "missing person" ads in newspapers for Knickerbocker, a "crusty Dutch historian," then published *A History of New York* "by" him.
- His family business collapsed after the **War of 1812**, and he declared **bankruptcy** in England. He wrote "Rip Van Winkle" **overnight** at his sister's house in Birmingham, England.
- Fought **bootleggers** (illegal copies), since there was no international copyright law. He pushed for copyright laws for American writers.
- Lived in Europe for **17 years**. Later he was U.S. Minister to Spain (1842–46).
- He was the **first American to earn a living only by writing**. He "perfected the American short story," wrote to **entertain rather than teach (not didactic)**, and **influenced** Hawthorne, Melville, Longfellow, and Poe.

**How his life shaped "Rip Van Winkle":**

| Life fact | Where it shows up in the story |
|---|---|
| Boyhood trips through the Catskills and Dutch ghost stories | The setting, the Dutch village, and the legend of Hudson's ghost crew |
| The Knickerbocker hoax / love of pseudonyms | The Preface pretends the story comes from Knickerbocker's papers. The "is it true?" game continues. |
| Born as the Revolution ended; named after Washington | King George's sign becomes "GENERAL WASHINGTON." The story is about the change from colony to nation. |
| Living abroad for 17 years, then returning to a changed America | Rip's 20-year absence and his shock at a changed country mirror Irving's own experience as an outsider. |
| Bankruptcy and failure | Rip as a failed farmer who still finds happiness. A gentle look at a man who doesn't fit the "work hard and get rich" model. |
| Wrote to entertain, not to preach | Humor, a tall tale, and no clear moral lesson |
| Read Dutch and German folk tales | The story is borrowed from a German folk tale (Peter Klaus) and moved to America. That's Romantic interest in **folklore**. |

## 6. Historical context (when and where it was written)

- **Written in 1819 in England**, about 40 years after the Revolution. America was still a **young nation** trying to prove it had its own culture and literature.
- **Romanticism** (packet pp. 1–2) was sweeping Europe. It rejected the order and reason of the **Enlightenment / Neoclassicism** and valued **nature, emotion, imagination, the individual, folklore, national origins, and the supernatural**. The packet notes the second phase (1805–1830s) focused on **cultural nationalism**, collecting each nation's folk tales. Irving does that for America with a Dutch-American legend.
- **The Revolution and party politics:** the story's "Federal or Democrat?" refers to the **Federalist vs. Democratic-Republican** party fights of the early republic. Irving shows that political change can feel loud and confusing for ordinary people.
- **Effect on the story:** Irving uses one man's sleep to show the **whole country's transformation**, but he's skeptical. Rip simply swaps "one species of despotism" (his wife) for freedom, and "petticoat government" was the tyranny he really cared about. Ordinary life doesn't change much.

## 7. Literary devices (using your class terms list)

| Device | Example (page) | Effect / why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Frame narrative / persona** | Preface "found among the papers of the late Diedrich Knickerbocker" (p. 31) | Makes a tall tale look like real history. It's Irving's joke and adds **ambiguity**: is it true? |
| **Imagery** | "magical hues and shapes," mountains "clothed in blue and purple" with a "crown of glory" (p. 33) | Beautiful, Romantic nature, and it signals magic is coming. |
| **Personification** | The mountains "lording it over the surrounding country" (p. 33); the Hudson "moving on its silent but majestic course" (p. 39) | Nature seems powerful and alive, a big Romantic idea. |
| **Foreshadowing** | Mountains called "perfect barometers" and "fairy mountains"; the ninepins sound like "distant thunder" (pp. 33, 40) | Hints at the supernatural and links the thunder to the legend at the end. |
| **Allusion** | George III, George Washington, Stony Point, Hendrick Hudson and the *Half-moon* (pp. 44–49) | Ties the tale to real American history. |
| **Symbol** | The **inn sign** (King George → Washington); the **liberty pole** replacing the tree; Rip's **rusty gun** | The sign shows the government changed but the "face" (people) didn't much. The rusty gun marks the passing of time. |
| **Situational irony** | Rip, who never cared about politics, gets accused of being a Tory spy. The lazy man becomes respected in old age. | Pokes fun at how seriously the new citizens take politics. |
| **Verbal irony / satire** | The "patriarch" Vedder judging the news by puffs of smoke; the "self-important man in the cocked hat" (pp. 37, 46) | **Satire** of small-town politics and self-important leaders. |
| **Hyperbole** | His fences "continually falling to pieces," his farm the "most pestilent little piece of ground in the whole country" (p. 35) | Comic exaggeration of Rip's laziness. |
| **Aphorism** | "a sharp tongue is the only edged tool that grows keener with constant use" (p. 37) | Witty "truth" about his wife and a classic Irving one-liner. |
| **Metaphor** | Calls marriage "the yoke of matrimony" and his wife's rule "petticoat government" / "despotism" (p. 50) | Compares his marriage to a political tyranny, which parallels the Revolution. |
| **Juxtaposition** | Old village (sleepy, drowsy) vs. new village ("busy, bustling, disputatious") (p. 45) | Shows how drastically America changed. |
| **Tall tale** | Sleeping for 20 years after drinking with ghosts | An outrageous, humorous, unbelievable story. That's in your class list. |
| **Ambiguity** | Rip's story "varied on some points every time he told it" (p. 50) | We never know if it's really true, which keeps the Knickerbocker joke going. |

## 8. Themes

1. **Change vs. staying the same.** The country changes completely, but Rip doesn't. "...the changes of states and empires made but little impression on him" (p. 50).
2. **Freedom and escape from responsibility.** Rip escapes his wife the way America escaped England. "Rip, in fact, was no politician... petticoat government" (p. 50).
3. **Identity.** Without his past, Rip doesn't know who he is: "I'm not myself — I'm somebody else" (p. 47).
4. **Nature and the supernatural** (Romanticism). The magical mountains hold the power to stop time: "these fairy mountains" (p. 33).
5. **Satire of politics and "progress."** New politics is loud and confusing: "rights of citizens — elections — members of congress — liberty" become "Babylonish jargon" to Rip (p. 45).
6. **Work vs. leisure (and the American Dream).** Rip rejects the hard-working, money-making ideal and is happy anyway. "aversion to all kinds of profitable labor" (p. 35).

## 9. Connection to the essential question

**Course EQ:** *How can we critically interpret the American Dream?*
**Unit 1 EQ:** *In what ways is literature a cultural artifact?*

- **American Dream:** the usual dream is hard work → success. Rip **flips** it. He refuses to work, loses his farm, and still ends up happy and respected. Irving asks whether the Dream is about **freedom** (Rip is free of his "tyrant" wife and the king) or about **money and progress** (the bustling new town that Rip finds confusing).
- **Cultural artifact:** the story preserves Dutch-American **folklore**, the moment America changed from colony to nation, and early **party politics**. It shows what a young America worried about: losing its past in the rush toward the future.

> **Model answer:** "Rip Van Winkle" is a cultural artifact of the young United States because Irving uses one man's 20-year sleep to capture the country's shift from British colony to independent nation, shown by the inn sign changing from King George to "GENERAL WASHINGTON" (p. 44). At the same time, he critiques the American Dream. Rip, who has "an insuperable aversion to all kinds of profitable labor" (p. 35), finds freedom and happiness without hard work. That suggests the new nation's busy, money-driven "progress" isn't the only path to a good life.

## 12. Likely test questions

1. **How long does Rip sleep, and what major event did he miss?**
   → 20 years; the American Revolution.
2. **Who are the strange men in the mountains?**
   → The ghosts of Hendrick (Henry) Hudson and his crew of the *Half-moon*, who return every 20 years. Their ninepins make the thunder.
3. **What is the purpose of the Preface about Diedrich Knickerbocker?**
   → It's a **frame** that pretends the story is real history. It's Irving's joke and continues his Knickerbocker hoax, adding **ambiguity** about whether the story is true.
4. **How does the inn sign show the theme of change?**
   → King George's face is repainted as "GENERAL WASHINGTON": new government, same face. Change is often surface-level.
5. **Name three Romantic characteristics in the story.**
   → Love of **nature** (magical mountains), the **supernatural** (ghost crew), **folklore and national origins** (Dutch legend), and the **individual** who rejects society's rules (Rip).
6. **Pivotal Question:** *In what ways does Irving connect Rip's escape from Dame Van Winkle with America's escape from England, and why is this significant?*
   → Rip calls his marriage "petticoat government," "despotism," and a "yoke" (p. 50), the same language used about British tyranny. Both Rip and America are "freed," but Rip cares only about his personal freedom, not political change. It's significant because Irving **satirizes** the Revolution's grand claims. For ordinary people, freedom meant something small and personal.

## Bonus: Packet discussion question answers

**Part I**
- **Q2. Define Romanticism in your own words:** A late-1700s to mid-1800s movement that rejected strict rules and pure logic. It valued feelings, imagination, nature, the individual, folk tales, and the mysterious or supernatural.
- **Q3. Define Gothic fiction in your own words:** An extreme branch of Romanticism that mixes horror and romance. It uses dark, creepy settings (old castles, ruins) and supernatural events to create "a pleasing sort of terror." Examples: *Frankenstein*, Poe, *Dracula*.

**Part II**
- **Q4. Authors Irving influenced:** Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, and Edgar Allan Poe (packet p. 3).
- **Q5. Two influential aspects of his early years:** (1) Trips up the Hudson and through the Catskills, hearing Dutch ghost stories, became the setting and legend of "Rip Van Winkle" ("the Kaatskill Mountains had the most witching effect on my boyish imagination"). (2) His brothers financially supported his writing and he loved the theater, which led to the *Morning Chronicle* letters and *Salmagundi*, where he learned satire and pseudonyms.
- **Q6. How travels affected his writing:** Living in England and Europe for 17 years exposed him to German and Dutch folk tales (the source of Rip). He wrote "Rip Van Winkle" in England. Spain's archives gave him *Columbus* and *The Alhambra*. His frontier trip produced *A Tour on the Prairies*.
- **Q7. Two impacts on American culture:** He popularized "Gotham" for NYC (later used in Batman) and "Knickerbocker" (the Knicks). He also shaped the image of **Santa Claus** (St. Nicholas in a flying wagon) and coined "the almighty dollar."
