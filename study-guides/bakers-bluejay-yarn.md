# Baker's Bluejay Yarn — Mark Twain (1880)

**📝 On the Unit 1 exam (Tue 10/6)** · **Genre:** tall tale / humorous "yarn," frame narrative · **Movement:** Realism / Regionalism (local color) · **Pages:** GrAmSS 137–141 (complete)
**Summary:** A narrator introduces Jim Baker, a simple miner living alone in the California mountains who claims he understands animal talk, especially bluejays. Baker tells how one bluejay tried to fill a knothole in an empty cabin's roof with acorns for hours, not realizing the acorns were just falling into the house. When thousands of jays finally discover the joke, they laugh for an hour, and jays come from all over the country to see it for three years.

---

## 🧠 Confusing Terms in Plain English
**Story words**
- **Yarn:** a long, exaggerated, entertaining story, usually told out loud.
- **Knot-hole:** a hole in wood where a knot (branch spot) fell out. *Your note: "piece of wood."* ✔
- **Comb of the roof:** the top ridge of the roof.
- **"Yo Semite":** Yosemite valley in California, a famous natural wonder.
- **Nova Scotia:** a province in eastern Canada (where the owl is from).
- **The mines:** the California gold and silver mining camps.

**Literature words**
- **Frame narrative:** a story inside a story. The narrator introduces Baker, and then Baker tells his story.
- **Tall tale:** an outrageous, exaggerated, humorous, unbelievable story (on your class list).
- **Anthropomorphism / personification:** giving animals human traits. The jays talk, cuss, gossip, and laugh.
- **Vernacular / dialect:** the everyday way regular people in a place actually talk ("chimbly," "a-swooping," "ain't").
- **Satire:** making fun of something to point out its flaws. Here it's mostly *humans*, through the jays.
- **Regionalism / local color:** writing that shows a specific place's speech, customs, and people (the California mining frontier).
- **Realism:** writing that shows everyday life and people as they actually are, not idealized.

**Big-idea words**
- **Gold Rush (1848–1855):** thousands rushed to California hoping to strike it rich. Most didn't.
- **Emerson's Idealist (your notes):** writing driven by ideas. You connected the jays filling the house to the gold rush.

## Characters
- **The narrator (frame):** introduces Baker and admits, "I never knew but one man who could [understand animals]." He's skeptical but passes the story along.
- **Jim Baker:** "a middle-aged, simple-hearted miner who had lived in a lonely corner of California, among the woods and mountains, a good many years" (137). He's the yarn-teller and the "expert" on jay language. Lonely, folksy, and a little unreliable.
- **The first bluejay:** stubborn, proud, hardworking, and hot-tempered. He swears he'll fill the hole: "I'm d—d if I don't fill you, if it takes a hundred years!" (139). Works himself "so tuckered out" (140). He's the butt of the joke, and a stand-in for an obsessed human.
- **The other jays (about 5,000):** a crowd that argues with "as many leather-headed opinions about it as an average crowd of humans could have done" (140), then laughs once an old jay looks through the door.
- **The owl from Nova Scotia:** the only one who "couldn't see anything funny in it. But then he was a good deal disappointed about Yo Semite, too" (141). The humorless tourist, and the final punchline.

## Plot
1. **Frame (137):** the narrator says animals talk, and introduces Jim Baker.
2. **Baker's speech on jays (137–138):** "There's more to a bluejay than any other creature." Jays have moods, great vocabulary, swearing, gossip, humor, and no principles.
3. **Setting the scene (138):** seven years ago, the last man in the region left. His empty log house has a plank roof and one big room with "no ceiling."
4. **Rising action (138–139):** on a Sunday morning, a jay with an acorn finds a knothole in the roof, drops the acorn in, and can't hear it land. He keeps dropping more.
5. **Climax of his effort (139–140):** after two and a half hours he's exhausted: "I've shoveled acorns enough in there to keep the family thirty years."
6. **(140):** other jays come, then more, until there are about 5,000 of them, all arguing.
7. **Turning point (141):** an old jay lands on the half-open door, looks in, and sees the acorns "scattered all over the floor."
8. **Resolution (141):** all the jays laugh for an hour. For three years jays from all over the U.S. come to see it, except the owl, who doesn't get it.

## Setting & Mood
A lonely corner of the California mountains near the mining camps, after the Gold Rush (Baker says the story happened about 7 years earlier).
- "listening to the leaves rustling so lonely in the trees, and thinking of the home away yonder in the states" (138): **lonely and peaceful**. Baker is isolated and homesick.
- "There stands his house,—been empty ever since; a log house, with a plank roof—just one big room, and no more; no ceiling" (138): **abandoned and quiet**, the leftovers of the mining boom.
- "They all came a-swooping down like a blue cloud" (141): **comic and chaotic**.
- Overall mood: **humorous, folksy, playful**, the total opposite of Usher's gloom.

## Author: Mark Twain (1835–1910)
- Real name **Samuel Clemens**. Grew up in Hannibal, Missouri, on the Mississippi.
- In the 1860s he went West and **worked as a miner in Nevada and California** during the silver and gold rushes. It didn't work out, so he became a newspaper humorist.
- **Life → story:** he knew lonely miners, mining-camp slang, and the habit of telling exaggerated yarns by the fire. Baker is basically the kind of man Twain met. The jay pouring endless work into a hole that never fills mirrors miners chasing riches that never came.
- He's famous for **satire** and **realistic dialect** (later *Huckleberry Finn*, Unit 3).

## Time Period (1835–1880, your research assignment)
Twain's lifespan begins in 1835, and the story was published in **1880** (in *A Tramp Abroad*).
- **California Gold Rush (1848–1855):** huge hopes, mostly failure. The "fill the hole" obsession fits.
- **Manifest Destiny and westward expansion:** Americans moving West to the frontier.
- **Civil War (1861–1865) and Reconstruction (1865–1877):** slavery ended, but racism remained (see the annotation about p. 140).
- **Transcontinental Railroad (1869)** and industrialization: the country is growing fast.
- **Yosemite protected (1864):** it became a famous tourist destination (the owl's punchline).
- **Literature shifts from Romanticism to Realism / Regionalism:** real people, real speech, humor, and less of the supernatural.
- Also the age of politicians Twain mocked ("A jay hasn't got any more principle than a Congressman").

## Key Literary Devices
| Device | Example (page) | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Frame narrative** | Narrator introduces Baker, then Baker tells the tale (137) | Makes the tall tale feel "reported" and lets Twain capture Baker's voice |
| **Anthropomorphism / personification** | "a jay is everything that a man is" (138) | Jays act like humans so Twain can mock humans |
| **Satire** | "as many leather-headed opinions about it as an average crowd of humans could have done" (140) | Mocks stubborn, foolish human crowds |
| **Vernacular / dialect** | "chimbly," "a-swooping," "you never see a bird cuss so" | Realism and regionalism: real miner speech |
| **Simile** | "like a 'possum looking down a jug" (138); "sweating like an ice-pitcher" (140); "like a blue cloud" (141) | Funny, folksy images |
| **Hyperbole** | "acorns enough... to keep the family thirty years"; "not any less than two tons"; 5,000 jays | Tall-tale exaggeration |
| **Situational irony** | Jay thinks he's filling a hole, but he's filling a house | The joke the whole story builds to |
| **Understatement / punchline** | Owl "was a good deal disappointed about Yo Semite, too" (141) | Deadpan humor, mocking humorless people |

## Themes
- **Stubborn pride and wasted effort:** "I've started in to fill you, and I'm d—d if I don't fill you, if it takes a hundred years!" (139)
- **Animals mirror human foolishness:** "otherwise he is just as much human as you be" (138).
- **Humor and community:** the jays "guffawed over that thing like human beings" (141). Laughter brings them together.
- **The frontier and its lost dreams:** the empty cabin left by "the last man in this region" (138).

## Essential Question: How can we critically interpret the American Dream?
Adams' Dream says ability and hard work lead to a fuller life. The first jay works incredibly hard ("two hours and a half" without stopping), but **effort without understanding gets him nothing**. He's pouring everything into a "hole" that can't be filled, like Gold Rush miners chasing wealth. Twain suggests the Dream can become a blind obsession. **Opposite of Rip:** Rip avoids all work and ends up content; the jay overworks and ends up a joke.
**Unit 1 (cultural artifact):** the story preserves mining-camp dialect, frontier loneliness, and post-Gold Rush disappointment.

## Connections
- **"Rip Van Winkle":** both are **tall tales** with a **frame narrator** (Knickerbocker / the unnamed narrator), humor, and satire of politics. Rip avoids work; the jay works pointlessly hard. Both comment on the American Dream and work.
- **"The Fall of the House of Usher":** both center on a **house**, but Usher's is a Gothic nightmare that collapses while Twain's empty cabin is a joke. It's a big shift from Romanticism/Gothic to **Realism and humor**.
- **"The Eve of St. Agnes":** elevated, poetic language vs. Twain's rough **vernacular**. Compare diction on the exam.
