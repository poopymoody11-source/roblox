## Key definitions
- **Chronocentrism:** the bias of believing your own time period is better, smarter, or more important than any other, and judging the past only by today's standards. *Example:* calling Irving's view of women "just dumb" without asking why people in 1819 thought that way is chronocentric. Good analysis explains a text in its own time *and* critiques it.
- **Transcendentalism:** an American movement (1830s–1850s, led by **Ralph Waldo Emerson** and Henry David Thoreau) that grew out of Romanticism. Its beliefs: truth comes from **intuition and the spirit**, not just the senses or logic; **God/the divine is in nature and in every individual**; and people should be **self-reliant** and trust themselves over society and institutions. Emerson called Transcendentalists **Idealists**: they believe ideas and the mind matter more than physical, material things.

## The American Dream (Aim: How do we define the American Dream?)
**Q: Deconstruct James Truslow Adams' definition. What is it, based on his diction? What is it not? How has it evolved?**
- **What it is:** a dream of a land where life is "better and richer and fuller for everyone, with opportunity for each according to ability or achievement," regardless of "the fortuitous circumstances of birth or position." Key words: *everyone*, *opportunity*, *ability*. It's about **equal opportunity and fulfillment**.
- **What it is not:** Adams says it is "not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely." It is **not just about money or things**.
- **How it's evolved:** today people often reduce it to wealth, a house, and a good job (material success), which is the version Adams warned against. Many also question whether opportunity is really equal.

## "Rip Van Winkle"
*Aim: How does Irving use characterization to develop a central idea / his purpose?*
- **Imagery and setting (pp. 33–34):** "every hour of the day produces some change in the magical hues and shapes of these mountains" and "these fairy mountains" create a **dreamy, magical** mood and foreshadow the supernatural.
- **1a. Notions of men and women (p. 36):** men (Rip and even Wolf) are sympathetic, easygoing victims. Dame is a nagging "termagant" whose "tongue was incessantly going." Irving casts women as controlling and men as free spirits who need escape.
- **1b. Does Irving favor one sex?** Yes, the men. Rip is "Poor Rip," and Dame is a flat stereotype who dies "in a fit of passion." She never gets her side told, even though she's right that Rip neglects the farm.
- **2a. Pathos:** "Poor Wolf... thy mistress leads thee a dog's life," "henpecked." Irving makes us pity Rip and laugh at Dame.
- **2b. Which do you favor? (opinion):** most readers like Rip, but a strong answer admits Dame has a point: the farm and the kids are neglected. *(Watch out for chronocentrism: judge Irving's attitude in its 1819 context, then critique it.)*
- **3. Commenting on the times (pp. 44–46):** the inn sign changes from King George to "GENERAL WASHINGTON," but the face is the same. The village is now "busy, bustling, disputatious," and Rip is called "A tory!" Irving suggests the Revolution changed **less in daily life** than people claim, and he satirizes the noisy new politics.
- **Reflect (Irving's life):** Catskill ghost stories became the legend. The Knickerbocker hoax became the frame. Living off his brothers and going bankrupt connects to Rip's "aversion to all kinds of profitable labor." Seventeen years abroad mirrors Rip returning to a changed country.

## "The Eve of St. Agnes"
*Aims: research vocab + identify devices; read for comprehension, not completion.*
- **What is the Eve of St. Agnes?** January 20th. St. Agnes was a young Roman martyr, the patron saint of young unmarried women. Legend: a girl who skips supper, lies on her back, and doesn't look behind her will dream of her future husband (lines 53–63).
- **What's the story and how do you know?** Porphyro, from an enemy family, sneaks into Madeline's castle, hides with Angela's help, wakes her ("This is no dream, my bride, my Madeline!" 363), and they escape into the storm. The plot shows through dialogue and action stanza by stanza.
- **Keats' message:** love is worth risking everything, but dreams are more perfect than reality, and youth and love are brief, framed by age and death (the Beadsman dies "among his ashes cold," 420).
- **Devices per stanza and how they help:** cold **imagery** ("bitter chill," 3) makes the warm love scenes stand out. **Juxtaposition** (old vs. young, cold vs. warm) carries the theme. **Allusions** (Merlin, "La belle dame sans mercy") make it feel like an old legend. The Beadsman **frame** reminds us everyone dies.
- **Connection to "The Haunted Palace" and Ethelred:** all three are medieval, Gothic, legend-like stories about a great house or castle and a woman in a deathlike sleep. Each ends with the "house" emptied or fallen (your idea: everyone runs away / the heirs leave / the house dies).

## "The Fall of the House of Usher"
*Aim: How does Poe's use of language convey his message? Why does Poe use other works of literature?*
- **Imagery and setting (p. 69):** "dull, dark, and soundless day," "clouds hung oppressively low," "insufferable gloom," "vacant eye-like windows" create a **depressing, anxious, dread-filled** mood.
- **Roderick (70–71, 74):** "cadaverousness of complexion," "ghastly pallor," voice swinging between "vivacious and sullen." He's falling apart like the house.
- **Madeline (77, 90):** "a gradual wasting away," then "blood upon her white robes." She's the buried family secret returning.
- **The house (72–73, 91):** "Minute fungi overspread the whole exterior," a zigzag "fissure," "an atmosphere of sorrow," and finally collapse. **Overall pattern:** every word choice points to **decay, darkness, and death**.
- **Message/purpose:** isolation and fear destroy the mind, and the mind, the family, and the house fall together.
- **Ethos / logos / pathos:** ethos = the narrator seems calm and trustworthy; logos = he explains things scientifically ("merely electrical phenomena," 86); pathos = the horror of premature burial ("We have put her living in the tomb!" 89).
- **"The Haunted Palace" (79–81):** the radiant palace = Roderick's healthy mind (windows = eyes, banners = hair, door = mouth, king = Thought). "Evil things" attack, and it ends haunted with "red-litten windows" and mad laughter. It foreshadows his madness and the fall.
- **Ethelred / "The Mad Trist" (87–89):** each sound (door cracking, dragon shrieking, shield clanging) is echoed by Madeline breaking out of her tomb. It builds suspense and makes the ending feel fated.
- **Why use other works?** They **mirror and predict** the main plot, add layers of meaning, and build suspense, even if the reader doesn't know the original source.

## "Baker's Bluejay Yarn"
**Q: What was happening in the world between 1835 and 1880?** (10 bullet points; double-check with credible sources and keep your links, as the teacher asked)
1. **1835:** Mark Twain is born; Andrew Jackson is president.
2. **1836–1850s:** **Transcendentalism** (Emerson's *Nature*, 1836; Thoreau's *Walden*, 1854).
3. **1838:** the Trail of Tears forces the Cherokee west.
4. **1845–1848:** Manifest Destiny; the Mexican-American War gains California and the Southwest.
5. **1848–1855:** the **California Gold Rush**.
6. **1859:** Darwin publishes *On the Origin of Species*.
7. **1861–1865:** the **Civil War**; the Emancipation Proclamation (1863); the 13th Amendment ends slavery (1865).
8. **1864:** Yosemite is protected by federal law, becoming a famous destination (the owl joke).
9. **1865–1877:** **Reconstruction**.
10. **1869:** the Transcontinental Railroad is completed.
11. **1870s:** industrialization and immigration boom; literature shifts from Romanticism to **Realism / Regionalism**.

**How it shapes the story:** the empty cabin, the lonely miner, and the jay obsessively filling a hole that never fills all reflect the post–Gold Rush frontier.
